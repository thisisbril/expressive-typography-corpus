operation_description: >
  The X's lower two strokes have been structurally modified. Their terminals curve outward and upward,approximating the bent-leg geometry of a running human figure. The upper strokes of the X remain intact, reading as the figure's upper body. All deformation occurs within the X's typographic boundary. The I has been rendered in perspective. Its left edge recedes and its right edge advances. Together they make the I reads as a three-dimensional rectangular slab viewed at an angle, approximating a door ajar. The I's fundamental vertical stroke geometry is intact. Only its dimensional state has been manipulated.
concept_encoded: A human figure running through an open door, the act of exiting
semantic_mechanism: >
  The X and the I are the word's two central letterforms and together they split the concept along its two components. The X's lower geometry is already a diverging form whose two strokes approximate legs in motion. Curving their terminals completes the running figure reading with minimal deformation. The I's rectangular profile is already the closest letterform to a door's silhouette. Rendering it in perspective opens it, transforming a flat stroke into a threshold. The operation enacts exit as a scene that consists of a figure in motion and an opening door.

curatorial_note: >
  A compound operation whose two primitives divide the concept precisely between two letterforms. The X carries the human act of running, leaving, moving through. The I carries the architectural fact. That is, the door, the threshold, the opening. Each primitive of the operation is doing the specific semantic work that the other cannot do. What makes this exceptional is the scene-completeness of the reading. Figure and door together constitute an exit more fully than either could alone, and the word's own letterforms provide exactly the geometry each role requires.
generative_principle: >
  When a word names both an act and the site of that act, examine whether two letterforms can be co-opted to carry one component each. Compound operations achieve their greatest semantic force when the two primitives are not merely additive but narratively dependent. In this operation, the figure needs the door, the door needs the figure, and the word needs both to mean what it says.
