operation_description: >
  The i letterform's stem has been reduced to a hairline stroke weight while every other letterform in the word retains the heavy sans-serif weight of the base typeface. The i's dot is preserved at or near the standard weight, creating a pronounced contrast between the dot's mass and the stem's near-absence. The i remains fully legible as an i. Its position, its dot, and its vertical orientation are all intact, but its stem weight is reduced to the minimum possible mark. The surrounding letterforms establish the word's base stroke weight, making the i's reduction immediately measurable by contrast.
concept_encoded: Slimness. The condition of extreme thinness relative to surrounding mass.
semantic_mechanism: >
  The i is one of the letterforms in "Slim" whose geometry is dominated by a single vertical stroke. Reducing the stroke weight of m, for instance, would alter one stroke among several. Reducing the stroke weight of the i reduces the letterform's entire physical presence to a line. The i suitably enacts slimness. Its body is slim in the same physical sense that a person or object can be slim. It occupies minimal horizontal space while retaining its full vertical extent. The surrounding heavy letterforms bring to light the relative condition of the slimness of the i.
legibility_integrity: Full

curatorial_note: >
  The retention of the dot at full weight is the piece's most intelligent detail. A fully reduced i would approach invisibility and risk the legibility that the concept requires. By keeping the dot heavy, the designer preserves the i's identity as a letterform while concentrating the slimness reading entirely on the stem.
generative_principle: >
  When a word names a physical condition of proportion or weight, target the letterform whose geometry is most fully constituted by the single property the concept describes. A letterform whose entire visible structure is one stroke is the ideal candidate for stroke-weight Mutation. Reducing or expanding that one stroke alters the letterform's entire physical presence. The surrounding letterforms must retain the base weight to establish the relational contrast that makes the Mutation legible as a concept. Slimness, like all relational conditions, requires a reference mass to be measurable.
