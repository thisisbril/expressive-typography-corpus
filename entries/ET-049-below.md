operation_description: >
  The l's upper terminal has been extended horizontally to the right, forming a ruled bar that spans the full width of the word. The bar sits between the two rows of the stacked layout, physically separating "be" above from "low" below. The l's geometry below the bar is intact. Only its upper terminal has been extended.
concept_encoded: A spatial threshold. "be" above the line, "low" below it
semantic_mechanism: >
  Below names a positional relationship, which is the condition of being beneath a reference point. The l is the operative letterform because it sits at the word's midpoint. It is the junction between "be" and "low" and its upper terminal is the geometric site from which the dividing bar can extend. However, the upward extension produces a critical legibility failure. The extended terminal and the bar together form a Γ shape. This resembles the profile of an r with a very long arm, causing the word to read as "berow" instead of "below" on first encounter. The designer's choice of upward rather than downward extension is explicable by lowercase consistency: extending the lower terminal would have produced an uppercase L, breaking the word's consistent lowercase register. The upward extension preserves register at the cost of letterform identity. The spatial payoff is real and the concept is enacted precisely — but only after the misread resolves.
legibility_integrity: Partial
surprise_legibility_ratio: 2
curatorial_note: >
  The most forensically instructive execution failure in the corpus because the designer's constraint is visible and their reasoning is recoverable. The lowercase-consistency requirement forced the extension upward rather than downward. It is a defensible decision but is still extracts too high a legibility cost. The l is both a letter and a divider, and the Γ it produces reads as an r before it reads as an l-plus-bar. The spatial payoff of "be" above the line, "low" below it is genuine and arrives within a beat or two of decoding. But the surprise can only arrive after the misread resolves. A correct execution would find a way to make the bar legible as a bar without making the l legible as an r.
generative_principle: >
  When a word names a spatial relationship and its syllables can be stacked to enact that relationship positionally, the dividing element between the two rows must be legible as both a line rather and a part of a letterform. A terminal extended to form a bar should read as part of the letterform before it reads as a bar unless the extension's geometry is sufficiently distinct from any existing letterform's arm or crossbar. The designer's constraint is a real design consideration, but it cannot be resolved at the cost of the operative letterform's identity. When the constraint cannot be satisfied without producing a misread, the design needs a different structural solution entirely.
